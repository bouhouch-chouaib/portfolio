## The problem

A company's employees use AI tools — writing assistants, note-takers, browser extensions — that the IT department does not know about. This is what is called **Shadow AI**. Every undeclared use opens an access path to company data and creates a hole in its compliance records, even though the company is legally responsible for every tool its employees use, including without its knowledge.

Aleth connects read-only to an organisation's Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 environment, builds an inventory of the AI tools actually in use, classifies them by risk level, and produces indicators and a report a compliance officer can act on.

The positioning is deliberate and constrains everything else: the product provides **indicators, never compliance verdicts**. The decision stays human.

The target customers are small and mid-sized companies of 50 to 300 employees, with a first circle in regulated finance. The buyer is a DPO or a compliance officer, and the trigger is regulatory.

## The constraints

- **Sensitive data that is not ours.** The product reads information about its customers' work environment; every access scope requested must be defensible in front of a CISO.
- **A real company, with direct competitors.** This page covers the problem, the standard architecture choices and the deliverables — deliberately nothing about the product's internal mechanics.
- **A team of two.** The product is built and maintained alongside my final year of studies: every technical choice has to save maintenance time.
- **Real billing.** A subscription flow in production, which means webhooks to make reliable and account states to handle properly.

## What went wrong

The product is sold on a promise of reliable indicators. Re-reading the customer journey, I discovered that when an analysis failed technically, the screen displayed an empty result — exactly the same screen as when an analysis completes and finds nothing. An alert did reach us on our side, but nothing distinguished the two situations for the customer.

In other words: **a technical failure read as a reassuring result.** On a product whose entire value is to reveal what you cannot see, that is the worst possible scenario — a false negative presented as a finding.

No test had caught it, because each function worked in isolation. The defect was not in the code, it was in what the code led people to believe. An explicit "incomplete analysis, run again" state, distinct from an empty result, was added before any customer deployment.

## What I take away

On a product that informs a decision, the absence of information has to be displayed as clearly as the information itself. Silence is never neutral.

More broadly, maintaining a product in production enforces a discipline no university project teaches: you don't choose your bugs, you don't postpone an incident to next week, and every line you add is a line you will maintain. That is where a rule I have applied ever since comes from: **I do not keep a line of code I cannot explain out loud.**
