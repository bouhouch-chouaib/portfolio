<Preface>
I wrote this text during my second-year internship, discovering from the inside how the systems everyone uses without understanding them actually work. It is about a gap widening between those who direct these tools and those who are subjected to them, and about what that does to a generation of students.

I am not writing it from the right side of that gap. I am writing it from the other side, and that is precisely why I can describe it.

This site is what I made of that realisation.
</Preface>

<Acte n="I" titre="The personal vertigo" sous="You introduce yourself, but not in the way they expect" />

Hello to you.

I hope you are well. No — I mean really well. Because in the world we currently exist in, getting up in the morning without dark thoughts is starting to look like an athletic achievement.

I haven't introduced myself.

I am a student. Not brilliant, not struggling: average. The kind of profile that fills lecture halls without leaving a mark on them. In an average programme, at an average university, in an average town. I am part of that vast silent army of young people still learning a trade, sincerely, naively, legitimately believing that this trade will still exist by the time they have finished learning it.

Future developers. Future nurses. Future accountants, financial analysts, web copywriters, paralegals, translators, drivers, surgeons. Future florists, even. That mass of people nobody really sees, who work, who struggle, who go into debt for their studies, who hope.

Did I say "future"? Wrong word.

Researchers — not the doomsday prophets, the real ones, the boring ones, the ones who publish in journals nobody reads — talk about 37 to 40 % of these jobs being profoundly transformed within five years. Not eliminated, no. Transformed. Which is sometimes worse. Because "transformed" without preparation means you miss the turn without even noticing. You keep training for a match whose rules changed at half-time.

But before we go any further: who is speaking, exactly?

Someone who has spent the last eighteen months immersing himself in AI almost obsessively. Not to show off. Not for a school project. Out of fear. Out of that particular vertigo you feel when you understand that something enormous is changing around you and that nobody in your immediate circle seems to have really seen it.

Do you know that feeling? When you watch a horror film and the main character is about to open a door the audience knows he shouldn't open? It's roughly that. Except the film is reality. And the characters are everyone around me.

I started reading researchers' reports when I arrived at my first company internship this year. Watching talks by people I only half understood. Cross-referencing data from completely different fields — economics, neurology, geopolitics, engineering — until a coherent picture began to form. A picture nobody had shown me at school. That nobody was really showing anywhere, in fact — at least not in a way accessible to someone like me. Because most people like me are simply anaesthetised by the endless use of their fingers to swipe from one video to the next. The only difference is that I like watching things people find boring or strange — mostly the kind of Arte documentary about the soybean production process in Japan. See, I did warn you. So once I had devoured two or three videos and articles on this subject, which was extremely vague to me despite the fact that I am studying computer science, that was enough to obsess me.

This manifesto was born of that vertigo. It is not a report, it is not a white paper, it is not a press article with experts quoted in italics to give an impression of rigour. It is a logbook. The logbook of someone who looked into the abyss and comes back to tell you what he saw.

You are not obliged to believe me. You are obliged to check.

To those who will try to find a point where I was surely too vague, where I didn't tell exactly the truth — I understand you, it's normal to try to reassure yourself. Ah yes… You're right… Florists won't be replaced, too much work with funerals. Well played.

<Acte n="II" titre="The invisible divide" sous="The two Frances, and why nobody really talks about it" />

There are two Frances right now.

Not the one of the rich and the poor — that's old, everyone knows it, everyone has made peace with it. No. A new divide, more insidious, more recent, and infinitely more dangerous because it is invisible to the naked eye.

Let me show it to you.

In the first world, there is a father who is an executive at a Paris tech company, or an entrepreneur, or simply someone who reads the right things, who started two years ago teaching his 12-year-old to prompt an AI. Not "doing homework with ChatGPT". Not "copy-pasting an essay". Learning to think with the machine. To ask it the right questions. To spot when it hallucinates. To understand why it gives one answer rather than another. To use it like a junior associate you direct — not like an oracle you consult.

That kid, in three years, arrives at university. He applies for the same internship as you. He is ten times more productive. Not because he is more intelligent: because he had two years of real practice that you did not.

In the second world — my world, probably yours — AI is a vague magic. A thing for generating funny images. A voice in a phone that makes you uncomfortable. An essay generator you submit hoping the teacher won't notice. Something that arrives, the way rain arrives, without anyone really having decided to care about it.

These two worlds cross paths every day. In the same lecture halls, the same open-plan offices, the same unemployment office queues. They speak to each other. They ignore each other without knowing it. And the gap between them is widening at a speed nobody has really quantified yet, because nobody has really looked yet.

This is what I call the invisible class imbalance.

That term — "class imbalance" — we instinctively associate with money. With postcodes. With elite schools versus everyone else. And yes, that divide exists, it is real, it has been documented for decades. But the AI divide cuts differently. It does not follow the lines of income or qualification exactly.

There are people without a high-school diploma who understand AI better than engineers. There are wealthy people completely out of their depth, and people of modest means who dived in out of curiosity and are five years ahead.

What determines which side you are on is a combination of three things: exposure — does anyone in your circle talk to you about it seriously? Activated curiosity — did you have, at some point, a reason to dig? And available time to learn — does your life leave you the space to take an interest in something that doesn't pay immediately?

These three elements are not distributed randomly across society. And their combination reproduces, magnifies and accelerates existing inequalities, while creating enough exceptions for us to tell ourselves that merit is enough. It is not enough. Or rather: it is no longer enough on its own.

Now, here is what really makes me anxious.

This divide is not perceived as a political emergency. It has no face. It produces no spectacular images for the evening news. It generates no demonstrations, because it doesn't hurt yet. Not yet.

The problem with invisible fractures is that they work underground. For years, sometimes decades, they accumulate a tension nobody sees — until the moment something breaks, brutally, at the surface, and everyone pretends to be surprised.

We have already lived through this with the internet. In the 1990s, experts said the "digital divide" between those who had internet access and those who did not would open up massive inequalities. And that is exactly what happened — except we did not see the second, subtler divide coming: not between those who have the internet and those who don't, but between those who know how to use it to produce value and those who use it to consume passively.

AI is replaying exactly the same scenario. Faster. More brutally.

The difference, this time, is the scale of the consequences. The internet redistributed access to information. AI redistributes access to cognitive capacity — and that is not the same thing. Information you can search for, find, digest by yourself if you have the time. Cognitive capacity augmented by AI — reasoning faster, analysing more data, producing more, deciding better — that you cannot improvise. It is learned. It is practised. And if you haven't started, you are already behind.

Let me give you a concrete example — not theoretical, real.

Today, a junior lawyer who knows how to use the right AI tools can do in two hours what a firm once billed as twenty hours of work. Contract analysis, case-law research, drafting first versions, spotting problematic clauses — all of it accelerated, augmented, amplified. Does that mean lawyers will disappear? No. It means firms will need fewer junior lawyers to produce the same volume of work. And that the junior lawyers who do get hired will be the ones who master these tools.

Same thing in finance. Same thing in journalism. Same thing in marketing, design, translation, customer support, accounting, strategy consulting.

This isn't a wave that is coming: it's a wave that is already here, and some people are already surfing it while others are still looking at the sea wondering whether it will really change anything.

And the worst of it is that the institutions meant to reduce this divide — schools, universities, vocational training bodies — are structurally incapable of responding at the speed of the problem. Not through incompetence, but by nature.

A reform of the school curriculum in France takes on average five to seven years between the political decision and its application in the classroom. Seven years. Consumer generative AI has existed for less than three years. Today's models did not exist in their current form eighteen months ago. At the scale on which AI evolves, seven years is several technological generations.

Meanwhile, the children whose parents have understood the stakes are practising at home. The children whose parents have not understood are waiting for school to adapt — or perhaps don't even know they need change. After all, a guy who does everything — your homework, your tests — is a dream come true. You can now cut yourself off from obligations, from classes you hate, from the misery of grades that for some pile onto the horrors this tutorial called life can produce. I think for them it's a real breath of fresh air — so how can you blame them? How can you blame a student today, a pure victim of this new world that does everything to drag them down, but blames them after landing a good blow to atrophy their brain a little further with all sorts of tools? But no: if they don't measure up, it's obviously their fault.

Forgive me, I'm digressing a little — school will adapt. But not in time. And that window between now and the moment institutions have caught up is exactly the period during which the divide solidifies. During which habits form. During which some brains learn to work with AI and others learn to work for it. This is not a metaphor. It is a trajectory.

<Acte n="III" titre="The cognitive casino" sous="How your brain was stolen, and why you signed the delivery slip" />

We need to talk about something uncomfortable.

Not AI. Not yet. Something earlier. Something that prepared the ground for AI to do what it is doing — and that nobody ever connects to the other, because it happens on different timescales, with different actors, in different contexts.

I want to talk to you about how your attention was stolen. Methodically. Scientifically. With a research and development budget NASA would envy.

Start with this: do you remember the last time you were truly bored? Not "I'm waiting for the bus and scrolling". Truly bored. Sitting somewhere, no phone, no screen, no music — just you and the emptiness. That uncomfortable emptiness that once always preceded something: an idea arriving, a daydream, a memory surfacing, a question appearing out of nowhere. For many of us, it has been years. Literally years. That is no accident.

Let's go back to 2009. Instagram doesn't exist yet. Neither does Snapchat. TikTok is an idea in nobody's mind. Smartphones are barely beginning to colonise pockets. And in the offices of Facebook, of Twitter, of the future platforms that will change everything, entire teams of psychologists, neuroscientists and behavioural economics experts are working on one very precise question. A single question.

How do we keep the user as long as possible?

Not how to offer them the best experience. Not how to be most useful to them. How to keep them. Because in the advertising business model, every second of attention is monetisable. Every extra scroll, every extra minute spent on the platform — that's revenue.

And to solve this problem, they drew on a century of behavioural psychology research and found the answer. It is called variable-ratio reinforcement — the mechanism that makes slot machines impossible to walk away from.

Here is how it works. Imagine you are playing heads or tails. Every time it's heads, you win a euro. The game is predictable: you can rationally decide when to stop. Now imagine a different game: sometimes you win on the third attempt, sometimes on the twentieth, sometimes on the second — and you never know when. Neurological studies show that this second game is neurochemically far more addictive than the first. Not because the winnings are bigger. Because the uncertainty keeps your dopamine circuit in a permanent state of activation.

That is exactly what recommendation algorithms do. You scroll. Most of the content is neutral, vaguely interesting, forgettable. And then on the twentieth scroll, or the fiftieth, you don't know — there's that video that makes you laugh really hard. That post that moves you. That piece of information that genuinely surprises you. That diamond in the rough. And your brain, without you asking it anything, records: keep digging. The next reward might be at the next scroll.

You can't stop. Not because you are weak. Because you are human. Because this mechanism was designed by teams of researchers paid millions to exploit precisely the flaws in your reward system. Tristan Harris — a former Google designer turned whistleblower — put it better than anyone: "Your phone is in your pocket, and there are a thousand engineers on the other side of the screen whose job is to break down your self-discipline."

But here is what isn't said enough. Attention addiction has a direct cognitive cost — this is not a metaphor, it is neurology.

The human brain has a remarkable capacity: it adapts to what we ask it to do — what we call brain plasticity. If you run every day, your cardiovascular system adapts. If you play an instrument, the motor and auditory areas of your brain physically reorganise. The flip side: if you spend hours every day consuming ultra-short, ultra-stimulating content that changes every seven seconds — your brain adapts to that too. It optimises for rapid stimulation. It becomes less patient with anything requiring sustained effort. Long reading becomes uncomfortable. Deep concentration becomes exhausting. Boredom — that emptiness I mentioned at the start — becomes unbearable, physically unbearable, because your brain has unlearned how to tolerate it. This is not laziness. It is reconfiguration.

Researchers at the University of California have documented that the average attention span on a screen fell from two and a half minutes in 2004 to forty-seven seconds in 2020. Forty-seven seconds — less than a minute before the brain disengages and looks for something else. And now, ask yourself the question: in this context — with a brain rewired for rapid stimulation, incapable of tolerating sustained effort — what happens when you add generative AI to the equation?

This is where it gets really serious.

Generative AI — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, all the others — is an extraordinary tool when you know how to use it. I am not going to give you the "it's dangerous, run away" line. That's false. That's reductive. And it is exactly the kind of discourse that makes people stop listening.

But here is what happens when a brain already weakened by ten years of the attention economy meets a tool that can think in its place. It delegates. First the small things: rewording an email, summarising an article you don't feel like reading, answering a question you could have looked up yourself. Nothing serious — real time saved, measurable efficiency. Then the medium things: the structure of a report, the analysis of a problem, the list of pros and cons before a decision. Here again, AI is often better, faster, more exhaustive. Rational to use it. Then the important things. The dissertation. The deep reflection. The reasoning you should have done yourself in order to understand — really understand, not just have the answer.

And that is where the slide becomes catastrophic. Not because the AI's answer is bad. Because the process of searching for the answer — of getting stuck, of fumbling, of being wrong, of starting again — it is precisely that process that builds understanding. That forms judgement. That develops the capacity to reason for yourself. When you use a calculator for 2+2, you don't lose much. When you use an AI to structure your entire thinking, you lose the thinking.

Here is an example I lived through — not theoretical.

A friend of mine — a law student, intelligent, curious — submitted a thirty-page dissertation generated for the most part by ChatGPT. He read the result. He approved it. He submitted it. He got a good mark. Three weeks later, at the oral defence, the panel asks him a question about one of the central arguments of his dissertation. He cannot answer. Not because he is incompetent: because he never built that argument, he copied it without going through it. He does not own its foundations.

Multiplied by thousands of students, across dozens of programmes, over years — what does that scenario produce? A generation of people with degrees but without the skills those degrees are supposed to certify. Professionals who know how to have things done but no longer know how to do them. Managers who approve analyses they don't really understand. Decision-makers whose judgement rests on foundations they never built. This is not a moral judgement: it is a mechanical observation. A system that certifies skills people no longer have eventually cracks. Not tomorrow. Not spectacularly. Silently, progressively, until something structural breaks.

And there is one last layer to this — the most perverse.

The AIs everyone interacts with were trained on our data. Our texts, our conversations, our ways of phrasing things, our verbal tics, our thought structures. When you ask ChatGPT to write something "in your style", it draws on a distillation of millions of humans who wrote before you. Alexander Wang — head of Scale AI, one of the companies that supplies training data to the largest AI models — put it bluntly: data is the new oil. Every interaction you have with an AI, every text you generate, every correction you make — all of it feeds the next models. You are not only the user of the product. You are also — for free, unconsciously — one of its raw-material suppliers.

And meanwhile, the companies that control this data, these models, this infrastructure — they are called OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI. They are almost entirely American. Their servers are in Virginia, in Oregon, in the Nevada desert. The decisions about what these AIs may or may not say, about what they value or devalue, about the biases they reproduce or correct — those decisions are made in San Francisco. Not in Paris. Not in Brussels. Not in Dakar. Not where you live.

That is the reality of the cognitive casino. You play. You sometimes lose. You sometimes win. And at the end of every round, the house — the house always wins. The question is not whether you want to play. You are already playing. The question is: are you going to learn the rules of the game, or are you going to keep pressing the button like a lab rat?

<Acte n="IV" titre="The state of the real world" sous="What is actually happening while you look away" />

I am going to ask you to make an effort.

For two minutes, forget everything you have read or heard about AI in the mainstream media. The articles about "will ChatGPT replace teachers?". The televised debates where two people who don't really understand the subject argue for seven minutes. The LinkedIn posts from people promising to "multiply your productivity by ten with these five magic prompts". Forget all of it.

I am going to tell you what is actually happening. Not in the press. In boardrooms, in research laboratories, in the corridors of defence ministries, in data centres that consume as much electricity as entire cities. The real world of AI in 2026 — the one you deserve to know about.

When you want to understand where the world is going, follow the money. Not the speeches. Not the promises. The money.

In March 2026, a Paris startup closed the largest seed round in European history. One billion euros. For a company of about a dozen people. With no commercialised product. With no revenue.

That startup is called AMI Labs. It was founded by Yann LeCun — a Frenchman, which deserves saying — who co-invented convolutional neural networks, who received the Turing Award in 2018 — computing's equivalent of the Nobel — and who spent twelve years leading Meta's AI research laboratory before walking out following deep disagreements with Mark Zuckerberg over the direction to take. A billion. For an idea.

What idea? To go completely beyond the paradigm of language models like ChatGPT — not to improve them, to surpass them. LeCun believes — and more and more serious researchers share this conviction — that LLMs have hit a structural limit. They predict words, very well, with breathtaking sophistication — but they do not understand the physical world. They have no sense of real causality. They cannot plan in space and time the way a living being would.

What AMI Labs is developing is called a JEPA architecture — Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. Instead of predicting the next word in a sentence, these models learn abstract representations of the real world from visual, audio and sensory streams. They understand physics. They anticipate the consequences of an action. They plan. These are the foundations of genuinely intelligent robotics, of a medical AI capable of interpreting a clinical evolution over time, of assistants that don't just answer your questions but act in the real world on your behalf.

And the investors who put a billion on the table for that? They are not fools. Among them: NVIDIA, Samsung, Toyota, Temasek — Singapore's sovereign wealth fund — and Bpifrance, which represents the French state. Those players do not bet on promises: they bet on what they see in their own laboratories and in their own markets. What that billion tells you concretely is this: the people with the most available information about the real state of the technology think that what exists today is not the final version. That what we are living through right now — GPT-5, Claude, Gemini — is the rough draft.

Meanwhile, another battle is being fought. Less visible, even more structuring.

On 27 April 2026, Microsoft and OpenAI announced the end of their exclusivity agreement. OpenAI can now deploy its models on Amazon, on Google, on Oracle — anywhere. What looks like an innocuous press release is in fact an earthquake. For years, if you wanted access to OpenAI's models in a professional application, you had to go through Microsoft Azure. That was the condition. The package. This forced coupling had made Microsoft unavoidable in enterprise AI adoption and had generated growing tensions with European and American regulators who saw it as disguised monopolistic consolidation. Now that lock is gone, and it changes everything for thousands of companies that can now choose their infrastructure independently of their AI tools.

But here is what is even more interesting than the agreement itself: the clause that was removed. In the original contract between Microsoft and OpenAI, there was a clause stipulating that the agreement became void if OpenAI reached AGI — Artificial General Intelligence, an AI capable of doing everything a human can do intellectually. That clause was OpenAI's only real lever of independence, and it has just been removed as part of the renegotiation. Why does that matter? Because it means OpenAI and Microsoft collectively decided that the question of AGI — its definition, its attainment, its contractual consequences — is too uncertain, too complex, too politically charged to remain in a commercial contract. They preferred to erase it rather than manage it. That is not reassuring. It is revealing.

Now here is what nobody tells you in the mainstream media. Because it's too technical, they say. Because it would frighten people, others say. Because they didn't have access to the information, more probably.

On 7 April 2026, Anthropic — the company behind Claude, considered one of the most serious on AI safety questions — announced something unusual. It had developed a new model of unprecedented power. And it decided not to release it publicly. That model is called Claude Mythos. No consumer version. No open API. No demo on the site. Nothing.

Why? Because that model is capable of doing something the previous ones could not — at least not at that scale: identifying unknown security flaws in complex computer systems, autonomously, without a human telling it where to look. Flaws known as zero-days — vulnerabilities nobody has yet discovered, which therefore have no available patch, and which can be exploited immediately by anyone who knows about them.

During testing, Claude Mythos unearthed a flaw dormant for twenty-seven years in the source code of OpenBSD — an operating system reputed to be one of the most secure in the world. Twenty-seven years. That flaw had existed since 1999. Nobody had found it. The model identified it in a matter of hours. It also detected critical vulnerabilities in the Linux kernel — the system that runs almost all of the world's internet servers, supercomputers, and the critical infrastructure of dozens of countries. To understand what that means concretely: banking systems, power grids, hospitals, rail transport systems — all of it runs partly on foundations that this model can scan and dissect in a few hours.

Faced with this, Anthropic created what they call Project Glasswing — an ultra-restricted consortium of about forty organisations with access to Mythos in a controlled framework: Apple, Google, AWS, Microsoft, Cisco, CrowdStrike, the Linux Foundation. The official objective: to allow the guardians of critical infrastructure to fix their own flaws before a malicious actor develops something equivalent. The Pentagon — which had initially classified Anthropic as a "supply chain risk" — ultimately approved the use of Mythos to secure its own government networks. The national security imperative took precedence over ideological reservations.

India formally protested against its exclusion from this circle — a leading computing power, with hundreds of millions of internet users, massive banking systems, critical digital infrastructure, excluded from the club that audits the flaws of global infrastructure. And the IMF — the International Monetary Fund, the institution that monitors global financial stability — issued a formal alert: if a malicious actor develops an equivalent of Mythos and uses it to target software libraries common to thousands of banks simultaneously, global payment systems could be paralysed in a coordinated fashion. This is not science fiction. It is a formal risk assessment produced by an institution whose role is precisely not to dramatise.

In response to Anthropic's withholding of Mythos, OpenAI reacted on 11 May 2026 with the launch of Daybreak — a cybersecurity initiative built on GPT-5.5. Sam Altman, on a podcast, did not mince his words about Anthropic: he described their communication as "fear-based marketing" — selling a hundred-million-dollar nuclear shelter while keeping the bomb for yourself. Daybreak's approach is radically different: rather than withholding, dissemination. GPT-5.5-Cyber — the most powerful version — is made accessible to verified security teams, integrated directly into the tools of Cloudflare, Cisco, CrowdStrike. The idea: to arm the entire industrial fabric rather than concentrate power in a closed club. Independent evaluations by the British AI Security Institute put GPT-5.5-Cyber slightly above Mythos on expert-level cybersecurity tasks — 71.4 % success against 68.6 %. It solved a complex reverse-engineering challenge in ten minutes and twenty-two seconds at a cost of 1.73 dollars. One dollar seventy-three. For a task that would have taken a human expert hours. That figure — more than any other in this text — tells you something essential about the world we are entering.

I have thrown a lot of figures at you. A lot of names. A lot of events. Not to impress you: to show you the texture of what is actually happening.

While French public debate revolves around "are we allowed to use ChatGPT at school", private American companies are restructuring the architecture of global information. They are building tools so powerful that they decide themselves — without a vote, without a parliament, without public consultation — which ones deserve to be deployed and which must be hidden. They form private consortia to manage risks that concern the infrastructure of entire countries. They negotiate with armies. They alert central banks.

These are decisions that concern you directly. That concern your data, your digital security, the stability of the banking system your salary will be paid into, the hospital infrastructure that will treat you. And you were not consulted. Neither was I. Nobody on our side was. This is not a call to panic. It is a call to awareness. The difference between being subjected to this world and participating in it — even modestly, even at the scale of one individual — starts with understanding it. And you are in the process of understanding it. Keep going.

<Acte n="V" titre="The four possible futures" sous="Choosing is not a metaphor — it is literally what is happening right now" />

We are at a crossroads. Not metaphorically: literally. The decisions being made right now — in laboratories, in parliaments, in classrooms, in the individual choices of millions of people — will determine which of these four futures we will inhabit.

I am going to present all four to you. Honestly. Without telling you which to choose, because that is not my role. My role is to make sure you really see them. That you don't find yourself in one of them by default, through inattention, because nobody warned you the crossroads existed.

### Scenario 1 — Augmentation

*The future we are sold, and which is genuinely possible.*

In this future, AI keeps its fundamental promise. It integrates into our lives the way electricity integrated into homes at the beginning of the twentieth century — discreetly, progressively, until it becomes so natural that you no longer see it, you only see what it makes possible.

The rural doctor who had no access to a specialist radiologist now has a diagnostic system that analyses imaging with an accuracy above the human average — not to replace the doctor, but to give them capabilities they could never have had alone. The first-generation university student — the one whose parents never went into higher education, who has nobody around them to guide them — has a personalised tutor available twenty-four hours a day, which adapts to their pace, which detects their gaps before they become blockages, which explains the same things in ten different ways until they land. The small entrepreneur in a mid-sized town can now access market analyses, legal strategies and financial optimisations that were reserved for companies that could afford consultancies at a thousand euros an hour.

In this scenario, AI is the greatest equaliser of opportunity in human history. It gives the smallest the tools of the largest. It compresses inequalities of access to expertise. It frees up cognitive time for tasks of true human value — creativity, empathy, judgement, relationship. This scenario is technically possible; it is already there, in seed form, in certain uses. Projects like Khan Academy with Khanmigo show what it can look like when the intention is emancipation rather than engagement. But this scenario does not happen by itself: it requires political choices, massive public investment in education about the tools, regulation that forces accessibility rather than concentration, and for the people who understand the stakes to actively carry it. Without that, this scenario remains a fine promise in San Francisco keynotes.

### Scenario 2 — The Divide

*The future we are sliding into without noticing.*

This one I am not inventing. I am describing it because it is already happening.

In this future — our current trajectory if nothing changes — the divide I described in Act II solidifies. It becomes structural. Irreversible at the scale of a generation. On one side, a class of people — not necessarily the richest, not necessarily the most qualified, but the best informed and the best surrounded — who master AI as a lever of power, who use it to produce more, earn more, decide better, learn faster, and who train their children to do the same. On the other, a growing mass of people subjected to AI — who see their sector transform without being prepared for it, who lose jobs not because robots replace them, but because a colleague who masters the tools now does the work of three people, and who use these technologies only as cognitive crutches, progressively impoverishing their capacity for autonomous thought.

The result in twenty years? A two-speed society — not between poor and rich in the classical sense, but between the augmented and the substituted. Between those who direct the machines and those the machines direct. What makes this scenario particularly dangerous is its invisibility: the divide will not be announced, it will not be the subject of a political speech or a newspaper front page. It will occur in the sum of millions of small individual decisions — a student who delegates their thinking to an AI without really choosing to, a parent who lacks the resources to support their child, a school that lacks the means to train its teachers, an institution that arrives too late. In this scenario, nobody is really guilty. And everybody pays.

### Scenario 3 — Runaway

*The future we don't dare look in the face.*

I am going to go where most people don't — not to frighten you, but so that you know this possibility exists and that it is taken seriously by people far more intelligent than me.

In this scenario, the rate of improvement of AI models does not slow down: it accelerates. The models become good enough to contribute to their own improvement: they help researchers design better architectures, identify their own flaws, optimise their training. This phenomenon has a name in the research community — it is called recursion — and its potential effect, exponential rather than linear improvement, is what has worried some of the most serious minds in tech for years.

Not the doomsday prophets: the researchers. Those who publish in Nature and Science. Those who worked at OpenAI, DeepMind, Anthropic, and who sometimes left those companies precisely because they were worried about the speed at which things are advancing. Geoffrey Hinton — the other Turing Award winner of AI, the one they call the "godfather of neural networks" — left Google in 2023 specifically to be able to speak freely about these risks. He said something that deserves to be quoted precisely: "I console myself with the normal excuse: if I hadn't done it, somebody else would have. But I'm not sure that consolation is enough."

In this scenario, the question is no longer "will AI transform the labour market". The question becomes "do we keep control over systems more intelligent than us in certain critical domains". And the honest answer — the one serious researchers give in private — is: we don't know yet. This scenario is not inevitable, it is not even the most likely in the short term. But it would be irresponsible to ignore it, because if we wait until we are sure it is happening to worry about it, it will be too late to change anything. That is precisely why organisations like Anthropic decided not to release Claude Mythos — not commercial withholding, but precaution in the face of a scenario they take seriously.

### Scenario 4 — Regulation

*The future we could build if we start now.*

This scenario is the least spectacular — which is why it is talked about least. It doesn't have the sex appeal of Scenario 1, the dread of Scenario 3, or the quiet resignation of Scenario 2. But it may be the most important.

In this future, human societies — states, international institutions, civil society — collectively decide that AI is too important to be left entirely in the hands of a few private American companies. And they act accordingly. Europe sets the tone — as it did with the GDPR on personal data, imperfectly but genuinely. The European AI Act, whose first major obligations come into force in 2026, imposes rules on transparency, auditability and watermarking of AI-generated content. It is an insufficient start — but it is a start.

States invest massively in sovereign infrastructure. France with Mistral AI — a French company developing models competitive with the American giants, open-source, accessible, auditable — shows that an alternative is possible: imperfect, limited in resources, but possible. AI education programmes are rolled out in schools — not to learn to use ChatGPT, but to learn to understand what a model is, how it is trained, what its biases are, why it hallucinates, how to question it rather than be subjected to it. Informed public debates — not media controversies, real debates with real questions — emerge in parliaments, in universities, in serious media.

In this scenario, AI does not solve every problem: it creates others, which we manage as we go. But it is governed: imperfectly, laboriously, contentiously, as all the great technological transitions in human history have eventually been. This scenario requires one thing the other three do not: informed citizens. People who understand the stakes well enough to vote on these subjects knowingly. To challenge their elected representatives. To refuse simplifications. To distinguish real progress from marketing promise. To carry these questions into their families, their schools, their companies, their neighbourhoods. People like you, if you have read this far.

I am not going to tell you that Scenario 1 is guaranteed if you work hard. Nor that Scenario 3 is inevitable if you do nothing. The reality is that we will probably live a combination of all four — some countries will experience more Augmentation, others will sink into the Divide, some sectors will see Runaway before Regulation catches up. The world is not a single path: it is a complex landscape where different trajectories coexist.

But here is what I know with certainty: the proportion of each scenario in that landscape depends directly on the number of informed people who actively participate in shaping it. Every person who understands these stakes and acts accordingly — even modestly, even at the scale of their immediate sphere of influence — pulls the landscape towards Scenario 4. Every person who passively submits pulls the other way. The crossroads is there. You see it now.

The most powerful tool you have against this revolution is not ChatGPT. It is your brain. Keep it. Train it. Use AI to augment it, never to replace it.
